# 12. Power in Your Mouth

---

Book: "Moc w gębie" [Power in Your Mouth] by Tomasz Kammel. As far as I know, as of today 21.11.2026, the book has only been published in Polish.

## Tailor Your Speech to Your Audience

---

A good speech should have one central message that you can express in a simple sentence. If you cannot do that, your topic is probably too broad. Before writing, identify who you are speaking to, what problems your listeners face, what interests them, and what they will gain from your presentation.

Define your purpose as well. Do you want to teach, inspire, encourage discussion, change beliefs, or entertain? Then brainstorm ideas and remove everything that does not support your main message.

**Focus primarily on your audience, not yourself.** Instead of concentrating on what you know, show listeners why that knowledge matters to them. Use simple language suited to their level of experience.

## Create a Strong Opening and Ending

---

The first few seconds of a presentation strongly influence how people perceive you. Walk in calmly, make eye contact, smile, and give yourself a moment before speaking.

Do not start with a lengthy introduction, apologies for being nervous, or excuses about being unprepared. Capture the audience's interest first. You can use a surprising question, an engaging story, an unexpected statistic, or a short riddle.

In the main part, present your strongest arguments, examples, and conclusions. Combine logical explanations with credibility and emotion. Structure stories simply: situation, problem, development, and resolution. People tend to remember specific stories more easily than abstract information alone.

**Plan your ending as carefully as your opening.** Leave listeners with a memorable conclusion, an important lesson, or a clear call to action. Do not automatically end with “Thank you for your attention” simply because you have not prepared a stronger finish.

## Keep Your Audience Engaged

---

Listeners will not remain equally focused throughout an entire talk. Watch their reactions and adjust your delivery when their attention starts to fade. Ask questions, invite brief discussion, use examples, and return to your central message.

Avoid predictability. Instead of presenting one fact after another, use stories, contrasts, surprising information, and thought-provoking questions.

Humor can build rapport and strengthen your message, but it should fit the topic and situation. Do not memorize random jokes. Draw on natural observations, amusing comparisons, and a little self-deprecation. Do not make jokes at the audience's expense or try to be funny at all costs.

Remember that your audience came primarily to gain something valuable, not merely to watch a performance.

## Manage Stage Fright and Build Confidence

---

Even experienced speakers can feel stage fright. You do not need to eliminate it completely. Learn to speak despite feeling nervous, rather than treating nervousness as proof that you cannot succeed.

Before speaking, prepare, rehearse the key sections, breathe calmly, move around, and drink some water. You can also try interpreting the feeling of heightened energy as excitement rather than a threat.

During your talk, focus on your message and listeners, not on analyzing your anxiety. If you lose your train of thought, stop, pause, and return to your next point. Looking at a friendly face in the audience may help.

Do not apologize merely for being nervous, and do not dwell on every small mistake. Most listeners will not scrutinize your behavior as closely as you do.

Build lasting confidence through self-awareness, acceptance of your limitations, trust in your decisions, and repeated experience. Looking confident cannot replace genuine skills and preparation.

## Use Body Language Effectively

---

Your posture, facial expressions, and gestures should support what you say rather than distract from it. Stand firmly on both feet, straighten your posture, and keep your hands relaxed and ready to gesture.

Gesture naturally. Use your hands to illustrate numbers, sizes, directions, and emotions. Avoid nervously fiddling with objects, constantly adjusting your clothes, or making chaotic movements.

Move around the stage deliberately. Take a few calm steps, stop, and continue speaking. You can change position when introducing a new topic or use movement to illustrate a story. Do not pace back and forth continuously.

Maintain eye contact with the entire audience. For larger groups, divide the room into left, center, and right sections, then gradually move your gaze between them. Do not stare at one person for too long.

Dress neatly and appropriately for the occasion, choosing clothes that allow you to move freely and feel comfortable.

## Speak Clearly, Calmly, and Concisely

---

During a presentation, speak a little more slowly than you would in an ordinary conversation. This gives listeners time to process information and gives you a chance to observe their reactions and control your delivery.

**Use silence instead of filler words.** Expressions such as “um,” “uh,” “like,” “basically,” and “you know” often add nothing to your message. When you need a moment to think, pause.

Practice clear pronunciation by reading aloud every day and repeating tongue twisters. Start slowly and carefully, then gradually increase your speed. Before speaking, relax your facial muscles and warm up your voice and articulators.

Avoid unnecessarily complicated words, repetition, and language mistakes. Do not say redundant phrases such as “repeat again,” “return back,” or “continue on.” Use vocabulary that listeners can understand without extra effort.

If you make a mistake and someone corrects you, listen, acknowledge the correction if it is valid, and move on. Avoid unnecessary explanations and arguments.

## Remember Your Speech and Practice Improvisation

---

Do not memorize your speech word for word. Remember its main sections, their order, and the key arguments. You will sound more natural and find it easier to recover if you lose your place.

You can use a **memory palace**: imagine a familiar building and assign each part of your speech to a different room. Create vivid associations, then mentally walk through the building to recall each topic in order.

You can bring brief notes with keywords on stage, preferably written in large print. Use them as reminders rather than reading the entire speech.

For spontaneous speaking, use the **PUFA framework** (a Polish acronym):

1. **Presentation (Przedstawienie):** State your position or main point.
2. **Reasoning (Uzasadnienie):** Explain why you hold that view.
3. **Facts (Fakty):** Support it with an argument, story, or example.
4. **Appeal (Apel):** End with a conclusion or a message to the audience.

Practice improvisation in everyday conversations, meetings, and short presentations. You do not have to start by speaking to a large crowd.

## Learn to Listen to Others

---

A good speaker should also be a good listener. Do not interrupt or prepare your reply before the other person finishes speaking. Maintain natural eye contact, ask questions, and occasionally summarize what you have heard in your own words.

Short phrases such as “So you mean…” or “Am I understanding correctly that…” can help. They show interest and allow you to check whether you have understood the other person.

Remember the names of people you meet. Repeat a person's name after they introduce themselves and connect it with a distinctive feature, association, or someone you already know.

Do not try to impress everyone. Show genuine curiosity and give others space to speak.

## Design Simple Presentations

---

Slides should support your speech, not replace it. Prepare your talk first, then decide which information needs to be visualized.

A useful guideline is the **10-20-30 rule**: around 10 slides, a 20-minute presentation, and a font size of at least 30 points. Do not treat it as a rigid requirement. Readability and relevance matter most.

Avoid long paragraphs, excessive bullet points, and complicated tables. Use large, readable fonts, consistent colors, strong contrast, and high-quality images. Highlight only the information you want listeners to notice.

Avoid unnecessary animations and effects. If a slide is so complicated that the audience needs a long time to analyze it, it contains too much information.

Before presenting, check the equipment, microphone, visibility of your slides, and how long your talk takes. Divide longer presentations into shorter sections that help refresh the audience's attention.

## Handle Your Audience and Difficult Situations

---

Building a connection with an audience starts before your first word. Get to know your listeners, inspect the room, and make sure people can see and hear comfortably from different seats.

Do not automatically interpret a neutral or unhappy expression as a negative judgment of your presentation. People may be tired, distracted, or preoccupied with their own problems.

If the room becomes noisy, stop speaking and give people a moment to settle down. If someone keeps asking questions, respond briefly, thank them for their interest, and suggest continuing the conversation afterward.

When someone asks a question you cannot answer, do not guess. Admit that you do not have enough information and offer to check. If a question comes from the audience, repeat it if necessary so everyone understands the context of your answer.

Do not shout, embarrass listeners, or get drawn into personal conflicts. Stay calm and guide the discussion back to your topic.

## Improve One Small Step at a Time

---

Do not try to improve every aspect of public speaking at once. Record your presentation, watch it, and choose the one area most worth improving: pace, eye contact, gestures, structure, or eliminating filler words.

Use the **Kaizen** approach of continuous improvement through small steps. Look for simple solutions, question the causes of mistakes, collect feedback, and put changes into practice quickly.

After every presentation, ask what worked, what did not, and what single change you will make next time. Once you have mastered that improvement, move on to another.

## Summary

---

A good presentation begins with understanding your audience and defining one central message. Create an engaging opening, a logical main section, and a memorable ending. Use stories, specific examples, and simple language.

Master the basics of voice control, gestures, and eye contact. Do not memorize speeches word for word, do not fear pauses, and do not let stage fright stop you from taking action. Listen to your audience, respond to their needs, and stay calm in difficult situations.

Record your presentations, analyze mistakes, and improve one element at a time. Confidence and ease in public speaking develop primarily through practice.
