# 13. Why We Sleep

---

Book: "Why We Sleep" by Matthew Walker

## How Sleep Works

---

Sleep is regulated by two main mechanisms:

1. **Circadian rhythm:** Your internal 24-hour clock regulates when you feel awake or sleepy. Light helps keep this rhythm aligned with the day, while darkness promotes the release of melatonin, which signals that bedtime is approaching.
2. **Sleep pressure:** Adenosine builds up in the brain while you are awake, increasing your need for sleep. Its levels decrease during sleep.

These mechanisms work best when they are synchronized. Night shifts, irregular sleep schedules, and travel across time zones can disrupt that balance.

Sleep alternates between NREM and REM stages. Deep NREM sleep supports physical restoration and memory consolidation, while REM sleep plays an important role in emotional processing, learning, and creativity. Deep NREM sleep is more common during the first part of the night, while REM sleep becomes more prominent later. Cutting sleep short can therefore disproportionately reduce these later stages.

## Why Sleep Matters

---

Sleep supports nearly every major function of the body:

- **Memory and learning:** It helps consolidate information and skills. Sacrificing sleep to study can make it harder to remember what you learn.
- **Concentration:** Sleep deprivation impairs attention, decision-making, and cognitive performance.
- **Emotions:** Sleep helps regulate emotional responses and cope with stress. Too little sleep can increase irritability and mental health difficulties.
- **Creativity:** During sleep, the brain processes information and forms new associations, which may help with problem-solving.
- **Physical health:** Adequate sleep supports immune function, metabolism, hormonal balance, and cardiovascular health.

Chronic insufficient sleep is associated with an increased risk of several health conditions, including diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and mental health problems.

Do not treat sleeping less as a productivity strategy. Extra working hours can come at the cost of poorer concentration, memory, and decision-making.

## Sleep Changes with Age

---

Sleep needs and patterns change throughout life. Infants spend a larger proportion of their sleep in REM, which plays a role in brain development.

During adolescence, the circadian rhythm naturally shifts later, making it harder for teenagers to fall asleep and wake up early. As people age, sleep may become lighter and more fragmented, making regularity and sleep quality especially important.

Modern life creates additional obstacles to healthy sleep. Artificial lighting, screens, irregular work schedules, and a culture that glorifies sleep deprivation can disrupt the body's natural rhythm.

## How to Improve Sleep Quality

---

1. **Keep a consistent schedule.** Go to bed and wake up at roughly the same times every day, including weekends. Allow enough time for sleep, usually around 7–9 hours for most adults.

2. **Optimize your bedroom.** Keep it dark, quiet, and comfortably cool, for example around 18°C. Block outside light, remove unnecessary light sources, and minimize distractions.

3. **Manage caffeine and alcohol.** Avoid caffeine later in the day, especially during the last 8–10 hours before bedtime. Remember that it is also found in tea, energy drinks, and other products. Alcohol may make it easier to fall asleep initially, but it can reduce sleep quality.

4. **Reduce light exposure in the evening.** Dim the lights and limit screen use before bed. Get natural daylight in the morning, ideally by taking a short walk outside.

5. **Create a wind-down routine.** Spend about 30 minutes before bed on relaxing activities such as reading, gentle stretching, a warm shower, or meditation. You can also write down your worries and one concrete step to address them the following day.

6. **Do not force yourself to sleep.** If you have been unable to fall asleep for a while, get out of bed and do something relaxing in dim light. Return when you feel sleepy.

7. **Address the causes of sleep problems.** If difficulties persist, identify possible causes and seek professional advice when needed instead of taking sleeping pills on your own.

## Start with the Simplest Changes

---

Do not try to change every habit at once. Start with three simple actions: set a consistent wake-up time, make your bedroom dark, and avoid caffeine in the evening.

Once these behaviors feel natural, add morning daylight exposure and a short wind-down routine. Notice how your energy, mood, and concentration change.

Consistency matters more than following every rule perfectly.

## Summary

---

Sleep is not wasted time. It is a foundation of physical health, concentration, memory, emotional regulation, and effective learning. Chronic sleep deprivation can impair everyday functioning and increase the risk of health problems.

Keep a regular sleep schedule, make your bedroom dark and cool, limit caffeine and alcohol, and avoid excessive light in the evening. Get natural daylight in the morning and create a simple bedtime routine.

**Note:** The book has been criticized for overstating some of the effects of sleep deprivation. Treat detailed statistics and highly categorical claims with caution rather than as unquestionable facts.
