# 10. Business Is Slipping Away

---

Book: "Biznes Ci Ucieka" by Marcin Osman

## Less Is More

---

Do not accumulate possessions and commitments without considering how they limit your freedom. Every purchase requires money, space, or attention. Invest primarily in knowledge, experiences, and valuable relationships. The less unnecessary baggage you carry, the easier it is to change direction when a better opportunity appears.

## Seek Knowledge, Not Gurus

---

Learn from people who have achieved what you aspire to, but do not blindly copy their decisions. Their experiences are subjective and may have been shaped by different circumstances. Check whether their advice fits your situation and today's market.

Before selling, negotiating, or starting a project, do your research. Understand your audience, their needs, the market, and available solutions. Gather information from different sources, compare it, and question your own assumptions.

## Meet People and Ask

---

Do not assume someone will say no. If a book interests you, contact its author. If you want to speak at an event, ask the organizer. If you want a better price or a partnership, speak directly with someone who can make the decision. Ask clearly and respectfully instead of waiting for an invitation.

Get to know people from different backgrounds, look for opportunities to start conversations at events, and volunteer for new initiatives. Speak openly about what you are working on and what you are looking for. A single conversation can lead to a contact, insight, or opportunity you would never have found on your own.

## Act Before You Feel Ready

---

You do not need an office, employees, or an investor to test an idea. Start with small experiments and explore different directions instead of making everything depend on a single plan.

The first version of a product, service, or book will not be perfect. Create it first, then show it to your audience, collect feedback, and improve it through successive iterations. Progress requires action, not just preparation.

## Customers First, Product Second

---

Before spending months building a product, check whether there is genuine demand. Find people who have the problem you want to solve, speak with them, and test their interest. The strongest signals are concrete commitments, such as orders or paid reservations, rather than compliments alone.

Adapt your product and tools to customers' actual needs. Do not build something merely because you like the idea or because a similar approach worked for someone else.

## An Idea Is a Starting Point, Not an Advantage

---

An idea alone has little value without execution. Do not keep it secret indefinitely out of fear of competition. Talk to potential customers, gather feedback, and test your assumptions. Your advantage comes from execution, how quickly you learn, and consistently improving your solution.

## Train Your Creativity

---

Spend 15 minutes a day generating a specific number of solutions to an unusual problem. For example, come up with 20 ways to acquire your first customer without a budget or 30 ideas for promoting an event. Do not judge whether the answers are realistic. The goal is to move beyond the most obvious associations. Only afterward should you choose ideas worth testing.

## Set Time Limits and Prepare a Backup Plan

---

According to Parkinson's law, work expands to fill the time available for its completion. Set short but achievable deadlines for tasks. First reach the "good enough" stage, then improve what truly matters.

At the same time, assume problems may arise. Protect your most important activities with a buffer of time, money, or an alternative solution. Fast execution and risk preparation can go hand in hand.

## Increase Your Chances

---

Do not treat success as a matter of luck alone. First, you have to participate. Then you can improve your chances through preparation: learn the rules, evaluation criteria, and competition; speak with people who have achieved similar results before; and find a way to stand out.

Look for niches with less competition, check application details, and make concrete attempts. At the same time, assess the cost of each opportunity. Not every victory is worth the time and money it requires.

## Stay Calm When Facing Problems

---

Fear often concerns imagined consequences rather than what has actually happened. Write down the problem and separate facts from predictions. Speak with someone who has faced a similar situation, remind yourself of past successes, and rest before making a decision if necessary.

Do not try to eliminate every difficult emotion. Learn to act despite uncertainty, and do not escape your problems through alcohol or unproductive worrying.

## Gain Experience Outside Your Routine

---

Travel and contact with people from different backgrounds teach flexibility, independence, and unconventional problem-solving. Speak with locals, observe how they operate, and learn even from chance encounters.

If you are learning a language, surround yourself with it every day: change the language settings on your devices, watch content in that language, and regularly speak with people who use it. Practice in real situations complements learning from books.

## Take Responsibility for Your Decisions

---

A leader can draw on mentors' experience, data, and the team's opinions, but cannot transfer responsibility for their choices to others. Gather information, make decisions, and accept their consequences. Indecision also has a cost.

## Summary

---

Do not wait for the perfect idea, product, or moment. Identify customer needs, start with small experiments, and improve your solutions based on real responses. Execution, not the idea alone, creates value.

Seek information, question assumptions, meet people, and boldly ask for opportunities. Train your creativity, set time limits for tasks, and prepare for setbacks. The more thoughtful attempts you make, the more opportunities you can seize.

Protect your freedom to act, learn from new experiences, and take responsibility for your decisions.
